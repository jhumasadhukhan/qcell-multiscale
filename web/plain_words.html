The quantum layer, in plain words

Twenty equations describe how a molecular sensor turns an invisible chemical signal into light a machine can read — and how the cell quietly corrupts that reading as it fills with plastic. Here is what each one says, without the physics.

Before any equations: what is actually going on

Picture two tiny lamps bolted to opposite ends of a molecular spring, all of it floating inside a living cell. Shine blue light on the first lamp and it lights up. But it has a choice: it can glow blue itself, or it can hand its energy silently across to the second lamp, which then glows yellow-green instead.

How often it hands over depends almost entirely on how close the two lamps are. Squeeze the spring and handovers become common — you see less blue, more yellow. Stretch it and the opposite happens.

Now the useful part. The spring is a protein that changes shape when it grabs the chemical we care about — a molecule called 2-oxoglutarate, which is the cell's internal signal that it is running short of nitrogen. More of that chemical, more grabbing, different spring shape, different colour mix.

So: measure the ratio of yellow to blue, and you have measured a chemical you cannot see. That is the entire idea. The twenty equations below are what it takes to make that measurement trustworthy — and equations 16–18 are where it goes wrong.

Equations 1–2How loudly can one lamp talk to the other?

Before energy can move, the two lamps have to be able to sense each other at all. These two equations put a number on that.

Eq 1

The strength of the connection

J ∝ (κ × μD × μA) ÷ (n² × R³)
connection strength = alignment × lamp-1 power × lamp-2 power, divided by how dense the surroundings are, and by distance cubed

Four things set how strongly the lamps feel each other. Two are properties of the lamps themselves (μD, μA — essentially how powerful each one is as a transmitter). One is how they are angled relative to each other (κ, equation 2). And one is distance, R.

Distance enters as R cubed, which is brutal. Move the lamps twice as far apart and the connection drops not by half but by a factor of eight. There is also n² on the bottom: the stuff between the lamps muffles the signal, and denser stuff muffles it more. Hold on to that — it is how the cell's plastic eventually spoils everything.

Why it's here: it is the foundation. Everything downstream is a consequence of this number.

Eq 2

The alignment factor

κ = (how the two lamps point relative to each other), κ² ranges 0 → 4
two aerials pointing the same way talk well; at right angles they barely talk at all

Think of two radio aerials. Line them up and the signal is strong. Turn one through ninety degrees and it can fall to nothing. κ is the number that captures that geometry, and it can swing the connection strength by a factor of four either way.

Why it's here: almost every paper in this field assumes κ² settles at an average value of about 0.67 because the lamps spin freely. Equation 11 shows that assumption is wrong here, which is one of the paper's quieter findings.

Equations 3–7From "they can talk" to "how often energy actually jumps"

Being able to talk is not the same as talking. These equations convert connection strength into an actual rate, and then into the fraction of energy that makes the trip.

Eq 3

The jump rate

rate = 4π²c × J² × 𝒥
how often energy jumps = (connection strength, squared) × (how well the two lamps' colours match)

Two factors, multiplied. The first is the connection from equation 1, squared — so doubling the connection makes energy jump four times as often. The second, 𝒥, is a colour-matching score explained next.

Why it's here: this is the engine of the whole model. In physics it is called a "golden rule" expression, which is just a standard recipe for turning a coupling into a rate.

Eq 4

The colour-match score

𝒥 = overlap between (colours lamp 1 emits) and (colours lamp 2 absorbs)
a radio only receives if it is tuned to the transmitter's frequency

Energy will not transfer unless the second lamp can accept the exact colours the first lamp gives out. Each lamp works across a range of colours, so this is the overlap between two curves — the shaded area where "what I emit" and "what you can absorb" coincide.

Both curves are scaled to enclose the same total area first, so the score measures match alone, not brightness. A perfectly tuned pair scores high even if both lamps are dim.

Why it's here: it is the one ingredient that cannot be measured directly in this setup, so equation 6 pins it down by a consistency argument.

Eq 5

The half-way distance

R0 = 0.211 × (κ² × n−4 × ΦD × Jov)1/6 ångström
the separation at which energy transfers exactly half the time

Rather than carry four separate properties around, the field collapses them into one summary number: the distance at which the handover succeeds 50 % of the time. Typically a few nanometres — a few millionths of a millimetre. For our pair it works out at about 5.9 nm.

Notice what is inside: alignment (κ²), the surrounding medium (n), how good a glower the first lamp is (ΦD), and colour match (Jov). Every one of those is normally treated as a fixed property of the sensor. In a cell filling with plastic, two of them are not.

Why it's here: it is the yardstick. "Half-way distance" is how everyone in the field quotes sensor performance.

Eq 6

A consistency check, not a fudge

𝒥 = 1 ÷ (4π²c × J(R0)² × τD)
two different routes to the same answer must agree — forcing them to agree reveals the one number we did not know

There are two ways to calculate the jump rate: the connection-strength route (equation 3) and the traditional half-way-distance route (equations 5 and 7). They describe the same physics, so they must give the same answer. Demanding that they do determines 𝒥 exactly.

The answer that falls out corresponds to a colour-overlap width of about 910 in the field's units — squarely in the range you would expect for two proteins of this kind. Getting a sensible number from a calculation that could have produced anything is evidence the whole layer is built correctly.

Why it's here: it is a check, not a fitted parameter. Nothing was tuned to make the results come out well.

Eq 7

What fraction actually makes it across

E = 1 ÷ [ 1 + (R ÷ R0)6 ]
at the half-way distance, half gets across; a little closer and almost all does; a little further and almost none

The number six in that exponent is what makes this technique work. Distance enters to the sixth power, so the response is extraordinarily steep: move the lamps 20 % further apart and transfer drops by roughly two-thirds.

That steepness is the reason a colour ratio can report a shape change far too small for any microscope to see. It is often called a "molecular ruler", and the nickname is earned.

Why it's here: this is the quantity the instrument is ultimately measuring, and the quantity equations 19–20 work backwards from.

Equation 8The genuinely quantum bit — and why it turns out not to matter

Eq 8

Does the energy hop, or slosh?

corrected rate = rate ÷ [ 1 + (2ωJ ÷ γ)² ]
if the lamps are strongly coupled and the surroundings are calm, energy sloshes back and forth like a wave instead of hopping one way

Energy can move between two molecules in two different ways. Usually the jostling of the surroundings scrambles things so fast that energy simply hops across, one way, at random intervals. But if the lamps are strongly connected and the jostling is slow, the energy can instead slosh back and forth coherently — wave-like, genuinely quantum behaviour.

This equation measures which regime applies. For our sensor the correction comes out at about five parts in ten thousand: completely negligible. The energy hops.

Why it's here: because it was calculated rather than assumed. Most papers simply assert the simple case. The term is kept in the model because for more tightly coupled pairs it genuinely matters, and then the simple treatment breaks.

Equations 9–10Connecting the light to the chemical

So far this is all about two lamps on a spring. Now we attach it to the quantity we actually want to know.

Eq 9

What fraction of sensors have caught the target

θ = Gh ÷ (Gh + KDh)
at low concentration almost no sensors are holding the chemical; at high concentration almost all are; KD is the halfway point

Sensors grab the target chemical and let it go again, constantly. At any instant a certain fraction are holding one. This gives that fraction: an S-shaped curve running from zero to one as the chemical becomes more abundant.

KD is the single most important design number — the concentration at which half the sensors are occupied. The curve is steepest there, which means the sensor is most sensitive there. Build a sensor with the wrong KD and it will be flat and useless across the range you care about.

Why it's here: it is the bridge from chemistry to shape. The paper's sensor-design chart is essentially a hunt for the best KD.

Eq 10

Averaging over a crowd

E(G) = (1 − θ) × Eempty + θ × Efull
some sensors are holding the chemical and some are not; what you measure is the blend

A cell contains many sensor molecules at once, some empty and some full, each glowing according to its own shape. The measured signal is the weighted blend.

There is a subtlety here that is easy to get wrong, and the paper flags it. You must average the brightnesses, not the shapes. Shape changes take about a thousand times longer than the glowing does, so each molecule glows as whatever shape it is currently in — it does not glow as some half-way average shape. Averaging the shapes first gives a different and incorrect answer.

Why it's here: it is the kind of detail that silently biases a result if handled carelessly.

Equations 11–13Why the sensor drifts as the cell changes

Here the paper departs from standard practice. These three equations say that properties everyone treats as fixed are not fixed at all — they change as the cell fills with plastic.

Eq 11

The lamps are too bulky to spin freely

κ²eff = ⅔ × fdyn + κ²static × (1 − fdyn)
textbooks assume the lamps tumble freely and alignment averages out; these lamps are too big and too slow for that

The standard shortcut assumes the two lamps tumble so fast that alignment averages to a tidy value of ⅔, letting you ignore geometry entirely. That requires tumbling to be fast compared with how long the lamp stays lit.

Here it is the other way round. These lamps are barrel-shaped proteins, bulky and slow: they take about 58 billionths of a second to tumble, while they stay lit for only about 4. So they are effectively frozen in place while they glow — only about 6.5 % of the averaging happens.

Why it's here: using ⅔ anyway is a well-known trap in this field. This equation replaces the assumption with a calculation, and the answer is nowhere near ⅔.

Eq 12

And they get slower as the cell thickens

tumbling time = (thickness × lamp volume) ÷ (temperature term)
spinning in treacle takes longer than spinning in water — and the cytoplasm turns to treacle as granules fill it

How long a lamp takes to tumble depends on how thick its surroundings are. As plastic granules accumulate, the inside of the cell becomes progressively more crowded and more viscous, and the lamps tumble more slowly still.

The second half of this equation is the thickening law — a standard relationship describing how a fluid becomes more viscous as you suspend more solid particles in it. The same mathematics describes wet concrete.

Why it's here: it is the first of two ways the growing plastic reaches back and alters the sensor's calibration.

Eq 13

The cell becomes optically denser

mix rule: n blends between ncytosol and ngranule by volume
plastic bends light more than cell water does, so as plastic fills the cell the whole interior becomes optically "heavier"

Refractive index measures how much a material slows light down. Plastic granules slow it more than the watery interior of a cell. Mix the two and you get a blend — and the blend shifts as the proportion of plastic grows.

Now look back at equation 5. The half-way distance R0 depends on this very number. So as the cell makes more plastic, the sensor's half-way distance shrinks and transfer efficiency falls — even if the chemical being measured has not changed at all.

Why it's here: this is the paper's central reciprocity in one equation. The sensor measures the process; the process alters the sensor.

Equations 14–18What the machine actually sees

Everything so far happens inside one molecule. Now the light has to travel out through a vat of murky broth and reach a detector — and that journey is where the real damage is done.

Eq 14

Two channels, plus some nuisances

ID = (blue light) × TD + background
IA = (yellow light + leakage + stray excitation) × TA + background
what the detector records is never purely what you want

The instrument watches two colour channels. The blue one is straightforward: whatever the first lamp did not hand over, it glows itself.

The yellow channel is messier. It contains the genuine handover signal, plus two contaminants: some of the second lamp gets excited directly by the illuminating light without any handover at all, and some blue light leaks sideways into the yellow channel because the filters are imperfect. Both are measured once and corrected for.

The terms TD and TA are the fraction of each colour that survives the journey out of the vat. Everyone normally assumes they cancel. They do not.

Why it's here: it is the honest description of a real measurement rather than an idealised one.

Eq 15

The ratio — and the assumption behind it

ℛ = yellow channel ÷ blue channel
divide one by the other and everything inconvenient is supposed to cancel out

This is why the whole field uses ratios. If the lamp dims, both channels dim together and the ratio is unchanged. If there are more cells, both channels brighten together — unchanged. If the detector sits further away — unchanged.

It is an elegant trick, and it works for anything that affects both colours equally. The next three equations are about what happens when something does not.

Why it's here: it is the assumption the paper tests, and breaks.

Eq 16

How murky the broth is

murkiness = aX × (ordinary cell material) + (aX + aP) × (plastic)
cells make the broth cloudy; plastic granules make it cloudier still, and that extra part is the problem

Light leaving a cell has to cross a dense suspension of other cells to reach the detector, and it gets scattered along the way. Two things contribute: ordinary cell material, and the plastic granules.

The granules carry an extra scattering term, aP, because they are dense particles of roughly the same size as the wavelength of light — the ideal size for scattering it. This single term is the villain of the paper.

Why it's here: it separates murkiness caused by how many cells there are from murkiness caused by how much plastic is inside them — a distinction that turns out to be decisive.

Eq 17

Shorter wavelengths get scattered more

Tλ = exp[ −murkiness × (λref ÷ λ)1.5 × path ]
this is why the sky is blue and why sunsets are red

Two things here. First, light loss is exponential with murkiness and distance — double the murk and you do not lose twice as much, you lose far more.

Second, and crucially: the amount lost depends on colour. Shorter, bluer wavelengths scatter more than longer, yellower ones. This is exactly why the sky is blue — blue sunlight is scattered sideways across the whole sky — and why the sun looks red at sunset, when its light travels a long path and the blue has been stripped out.

Why it's here: everything now follows. Our two channels are different colours, so they are not attenuated equally.

Eq 18 — the key result

The ratio does not cancel after all

measured ratio ÷ true ratio = TA ÷ TD > 1 (always)
the blue channel is dimmed more than the yellow one, so the ratio reads high — and gets worse as more plastic accumulates

Put equations 15, 16 and 17 together. The blue channel is at a shorter wavelength than the yellow one. Shorter wavelengths scatter more. So the blue channel loses more light on the way out than the yellow channel does.

The ratio is yellow divided by blue. If blue is suppressed more, the ratio comes out too high — always, never too low. And the error grows as the cells fill with plastic, because the plastic is what is doing the extra scattering.

The practical consequence is unpleasant. The control system reads the inflated ratio and concludes the cells are more starved of nitrogen than they really are. So it feeds them more nitrogen. But nitrogen is precisely what tells cells to stop making plastic and go back to growing. The measurement error causes the controller to do the opposite of what it should — and costs about a fifth of the product.

Worse, you cannot fix it with a turbidity meter. A turbidity meter tells you how cloudy the broth is, not how much of that cloudiness is plastic — and only the plastic part behaves this way. That number has to come from a model of the fermentation itself, which is the entire reason the two scales must be solved together.

In one line: dividing one colour by another cancels everything except colour-dependent effects — and scattering is the most colour-dependent effect there is.

An analogy for equation 18

You are judging a race between two runners through thick fog — one in a blue vest, one in a yellow vest — by how clearly you can see each of them.

Fog hides blue more effectively than yellow. So as the fog thickens, the blue runner fades faster, and you conclude the yellow runner has pulled ahead. Neither runner has changed speed. The fog did.

Now the twist that makes this paper's version harder: the runners are generating the fog as they run. The further the race goes, the thicker it gets, and the more confidently wrong you become.

Equations 19–20Working backwards to the answer

Everything above runs forwards: from chemistry, to shape, to light, to detector. To actually control anything you need to run it in reverse — from the measured number back to the chemical concentration.

Eq 19

Undo the instrument

ℛ′ = ℛ × (TD ÷ TA) → solve for transfer efficiency E
first remove the scattering distortion, then strip out the leakage and stray-excitation terms

Step one is the correction equation 18 demands: multiply the measured ratio by the scattering factor to recover what the ratio would have been without the fog. This is the step that needs the plastic concentration from the fermentation model.

Step two is routine algebra — equation 14 run in reverse to strip out the leakage, stray excitation and background, leaving the transfer efficiency.

Why it's here: the three versions of the paper's controller differ only in what they put into this one step. One ignores the correction entirely, one corrects for cell density only, one does it properly.

Eq 20

From efficiency back to concentration

θ = (E − Eempty) ÷ (Efull − Eempty) ; Ĝ = KD × [ θ ÷ (1 − θ) ]1/h
where does this brightness sit between "all sensors empty" and "all sensors full"? — then read the concentration off the binding curve

Two short steps. First, equation 10 reversed: given the measured efficiency, what fraction of sensors must be holding the chemical? Second, equation 9 reversed: given that fraction, what concentration produces it?

The hat on Ĝ is the convention for an estimate rather than the truth. The gap between Ĝ and the real value is what the whole paper is about: trusting the raw ratio makes that gap about 29 %; doing the correction properly closes it to well under 1 %.

Why it's here: this is the number the controller acts on. Every equation above exists to make this one honest.

The cellular-scale equations (21–36) describe the fermentation itself — how cells grow, consume formate, run short of nitrogen and make plastic. They are ordinary bookkeeping by comparison: what goes in, what comes out, what is left.
